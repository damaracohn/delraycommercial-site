Selling Commercial Property in Palm Beach County: A Guide by Building Type

Every commercial building sells on the income it produces and the risk a buyer sees in that income. What changes from one building type to the next is where buyers look for the risk. An apartment buyer reads the rent roll first. A warehouse buyer measures the ceiling. A medical office buyer wants to know how long the doctors have been there.

How To Use This Guide

This guide walks through each building type the way a buyer will: what counts as that type, how it is valued, what you will be asked for, and what tends to slow a sale down. It covers commercial sales from Pompano Beach to West Palm Beach, with a heavy focus on Delray Beach.

Building Type

Multifamily and apartment buildings

What counts as multifamily?

A residential rental building with five or more units. Lenders and most buyers treat five units and up as commercial property, and it is valued on income rather than on what nearby houses sold for.

How do buyers value an apartment building?

On net operating income: the rent the building actually collects, less what it costs to run. Buyers compare your in-place rents to what similar units nearby rent for, because the gap between the two is the upside they are paying for.

What will a buyer ask to see?

  • A rent roll showing every unit, the rent, and the lease start and end dates
  • Twelve months of income and expenses
  • The leases themselves
  • Insurance, property tax and utility bills
  • Roof age, and what has been replaced in plumbing, electrical and air conditioning

What slows a multifamily sale down?

Rent collected in cash with no record. Month-to-month tenants with no written lease. Expenses the owner pays personally that never show up on the building's books. Each one makes the income harder to prove, and buyers pay for income they can verify.

Building Type

Retail buildings and strip centers

What counts as retail?

Storefront property where tenants sell to the public: a freestanding store, a row of shops, or a neighborhood center with shared parking.

How do buyers value a retail center?

On income, weighted by how secure each tenant is. Buyers look at how long each lease runs, who the tenants are, and whether the center depends on one tenant to draw traffic for the others.

What will a buyer ask to see?

  • Every lease, with amendments and renewal options
  • A breakdown of what tenants reimburse for taxes, insurance and common-area costs
  • Tenant sales figures, where leases require tenants to report them
  • Parking count, signage rights, and any restrictions on what kinds of tenants are allowed

What slows a retail sale down?

Leases that all expire close together. A past or present dry cleaner, gas station or auto repair tenant, which leads buyers to order environmental testing. Tenants who are behind on rent or reimbursements.

Building Type

Office buildings

What counts as office?

Buildings leased to professional and business tenants, from a single-tenant building to a multi-story property with shared lobbies and common areas.

How do buyers value an office building?

On income and on the lease expiration schedule. A buyer wants to know how much of the building comes up for renewal in any one stretch, and what it will cost in improvements and leasing to keep it full.

What will a buyer ask to see?

  • Rent roll and leases
  • How the rentable square footage was measured
  • Parking ratio
  • Age and service records for elevators, air conditioning and the roof
  • What you have historically spent to prepare a suite for a new tenant

What slows an office sale down?

A large tenant with a short remaining term and no signed renewal. Suites that have sat vacant. Building systems near the end of their life with no reserve set aside.

Building Type

Medical office

What counts as medical office?

Space built out for clinical use: physician practices, dental, imaging, outpatient surgery, physical therapy.

How do buyers value medical office?

On income, with extra weight on tenant stability. Medical tenants invest heavily in their space and tend to stay, which buyers value. They will look at the type of practice in each suite, the lease terms, and how close the building is to a hospital campus.

What will a buyer ask to see?

  • Leases, including who paid for the interior buildout and who owns it at the end of the term
  • Any guarantees behind the leases, personal or by the practice group
  • Parking count, since medical use needs more than general office
  • Accessibility features and any specialized plumbing, electrical or shielding in the building

What slows a medical office sale down?

A practice that occupies the building and also owns it, with no lease in place between the two. If you plan to stay as the tenant after the sale, the lease you sign is what the buyer is really purchasing, and it should be worked out before the building goes to market.

Building Type

Industrial, warehouse and flex

What counts as industrial?

Warehouses, distribution buildings, light manufacturing, and flex buildings that combine office space with warehouse space in bays.

How do buyers value industrial property?

On income, and on how usable the building is for the next tenant. Function matters more here than appearance.

What will a buyer ask to see?

  • Clear ceiling height
  • Loading: dock-high doors, grade-level doors, and how many of each
  • Electrical service, including whether there is three-phase power
  • Truck access and turning room
  • Whether outdoor storage is permitted
  • Zoning and the uses it allows
  • Leases and rent roll for multi-tenant buildings

What slows an industrial sale down?

Environmental questions from past uses. Tenant improvements built without permits. Uses on site that the zoning does not allow.

Building Type

Triple net (NNN) and single-tenant properties

What counts as triple net?

A property, usually with one tenant, where the lease makes the tenant responsible for property taxes, insurance and maintenance. The owner collects rent with few operating duties.

How do buyers value a triple net property?

Almost entirely on the lease. The building matters less than who signed the lease, how long it runs, and what the rent does over time.

What will a buyer ask to see?

  • The full lease and every amendment
  • Who guarantees the lease: the parent company or an individual franchise operator
  • Remaining term and renewal options
  • Scheduled rent increases
  • Who is responsible for the roof, structure and parking lot. Some leases shift everything to the tenant and some leave those with the owner

What slows a triple net sale down?

A short remaining term with no renewal signed. A lease that leaves roof and structure with the owner when the roof is old. A guarantee from a small operator rather than the brand itself.

Building Type

Self storage

What counts as self storage?

Facilities that rent individual units to the public, drive-up, interior climate-controlled, or a mix, sometimes with vehicle or boat storage.

How do buyers value self storage?

On income, like other commercial property, with attention to how full the facility is and how its rates compare to competitors nearby. Because units rent month to month, buyers also look at how actively rates have been managed.

What will a buyer ask to see?

  • Occupancy by unit size
  • The unit mix, and how much is climate-controlled
  • Rental rate history
  • The management software reports
  • Whether there is land or building area to add units

What slows a self storage sale down?

Records kept by hand with no management system behind them. Rates that have not been adjusted in years, which makes it hard to show a buyer what the facility can earn.

Building Type

Mixed-use buildings

What counts as mixed use?

A building with more than one kind of use, most often storefronts at street level with apartments or offices above.

How do buyers value mixed use?

Each part is valued on its own income and its own risk, then combined. Some lenders treat mixed use differently from a single-use building, so the pool of buyers depends partly on how the income splits between the uses.

What will a buyer ask to see?

  • Separate rent rolls for the commercial and residential portions
  • How utilities and expenses are divided between them
  • Parking assigned to each use
  • Zoning, and whether the current uses conform to it

What slows a mixed-use sale down?

Books that blend the residential and commercial income together. Shared meters with no clear split. Upper-floor units that were converted without permits.

Building Type

Commercial and development land

What counts as commercial land?

Vacant parcels, and improved parcels where the land is worth more than the building standing on it.

How do buyers value land?

On what can be built, not on what is there. That is set by zoning, the future land use designation in the local comprehensive plan, and how much of the site is actually buildable.

What will a buyer ask to see?

  • A current survey
  • Zoning and future land use designation
  • Allowed density, height and setbacks
  • Access to the road and to water, sewer and power
  • Any environmental or wetland studies
  • Any approvals already granted, and whether they transfer to a new owner

What slows a land sale down?

An old or missing survey. Access that depends on a neighbor's property with nothing recorded. Approvals that have expired. Buyers of land usually need time to confirm what the municipality will allow, so the more of that you can document up front, the shorter that period runs.

Before You Call Anyone

What every commercial seller should have ready

Whatever the building type, the same core file answers most of a buyer's early questions:

  • Rent roll
  • Twelve months of income and operating expenses
  • All leases and amendments
  • A list of major repairs and replacements, with dates
  • Loan balance, maturity and any prepayment penalty
  • Survey, title policy and any environmental reports you already have

If you do not have all of it, call anyway. Rebuilding the file is part of the work.

Common Questions

Common questions from commercial sellers

Do I have to tell my tenants I am selling?

Not at the start, in most cases. Many owners keep a sale confidential until a buyer is under contract. At that point the buyer will need to inspect the property and will usually ask each tenant to confirm their lease terms in writing, so tenants do find out before closing. Check your leases for any notice requirements.

Can I sell a building with leases in place?

Yes. In most cases the buyer takes the building subject to the existing leases, and for an investor the leases are the reason to buy. The exception is a buyer who wants to occupy the building, who will care about when the space becomes available.

Should I make repairs before I sell?

It depends on the repair. Buyers deduct deferred maintenance from their offer, often at more than it would cost you to fix. But some work will not return what you spend. This is worth a conversation before you spend anything.

What is the difference between selling my building and selling my business?

They are separate assets. If you own the property and operate a business in it, you can sell both together, sell the business and keep the building as a landlord, or sell the building and stay on as the tenant. Each choice changes who the buyer is and how the sale is structured.

I am thinking about a 1031 exchange. When should I start planning?

Before the building is listed. An exchange has fixed deadlines that begin when your sale closes, so the replacement property search should be under way early. Use the 1031 exchange deadline calculator to see the dates, and speak with a qualified intermediary and your tax advisor.
